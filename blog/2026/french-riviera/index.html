🔌 Power adapter (US → EU)

💬 Prompt
Do I need a plug adapter going from the US to France?
✦ Gemini

Yes — traveling from the US to France you must use a plug adapter. US standard is 120V / 60Hz with A/B flat-pin sockets; France is 230V / 50Hz with C/E round-pin sockets. Two key differences:

1. Plug shape differs (adapter required)

  • US plug: two flat pins (Type A) or two flat + one round ground (Type B).
  • France socket: two round pins (Type C) or two round pins with a ground post (Type E).
  • Fix: buy a "US → EU" adapter, or bring a universal travel adapter.

2. Voltage differs (check your device) — France's 230V is nearly double the US 120V:

  • Phones, laptops (e.g. MacBook), tablets, cameras: their power bricks are usually wide-voltage (marked INPUT: 100–240V, 50/60Hz), so a plug adapter alone is fine — no transformer needed.
  • Hair dryers, curling irons, electric shavers: these heating tools are often fixed at 120V; plugging straight into French power can fry them or start a fire. Unless labeled 110–240V dual-voltage, you'd need a high-wattage voltage converter, or just borrow/buy one locally.

💶 Exchange cash?

💬 Prompt
Do I need to exchange cash for Nice?
✦ Gemini

You don't need much cash for Nice — cards (Visa/Mastercard, contactless widely supported) work almost everywhere — but keep a small amount of change (~€50–100) on hand:

  • Card works (the vast majority): restaurants, supermarkets, hotels, department stores, and public transit (tram, train) all take cards / contactless.
  • Where cash is handy: local markets (like the Saleya flower market), small street bakeries, low-value purchases, tips; some public toilets need coins.
  • Cheapest way to get euros: withdraw at a local ATM with a no-foreign-fee debit card. Don't exchange at the airport (poor rates); if you must, use a city exchange (e.g. Globex) and carry small notes (€5/€10/€20 — shops often won't break large bills).

🩺 When Things Went Wrong: Meds, the ER & Re-planning

Things still went sideways — one day I was biking by the sea along Nice's Promenade des Anglais, and then I fell. 😅 The self-care, buying meds, the ER visit, and re-planning the trip afterward all leaned on Gemini, step by step.

What Gemini helped with

💊 Meds & first aid (before the ER)

💬 Prompt
Can the white spray from the pharmacy reduce the toe swelling? What else can I do?
✦ Gemini

Whether the white spray helps depends on its type:

  1. Cold/analgesic spray (sports cold sprays, lidocaine sprays): if it feels icy, even frosting over, it works by rapid cooling to constrict vessels and numb pain — effective for acute swelling in the first 24–48 hours.
  2. Antiseptic/skin spray (benzalkonium, wound sprays): more for disinfecting/stopping bleeding — limited direct effect on physical swelling.
Check the label: "cold / sprain" → fine to spray on the swollen toe; "wound care / antiseptic" → better for the bleeding knee.

Beyond the spray, follow R.I.C.E. (Ice + Elevate here):

  • Ice: cold pack or a towel-wrapped frozen drink, 15–20 min, several times a day. No heat the first two days — it worsens swelling.
  • Elevate: prop the foot above heart level to help drainage.
  • Reduce load: walk as little as possible.

⚠️ Go to a hospital if you can't bear weight at all, the toe looks deformed, or the pain is unbearable — get an X-ray to rule out a fracture. Rinse the knee wound with saline/clean water, apply iodine, and keep it dry.

🏥 The ER: process & insurance reimbursement

💬 Prompt
I've decided to go to the ER — what's the on-site process for a foreigner, and is there French I can just show them?
✦ Gemini

📝 Foreigner ER process & key French. At the ER hall, queue at reception (Accueil) for triage.

1. What you'll need:

  • Passport — required, to register your identity.
  • Hotel address & phone — tell them where you're staying.
  • Payment & insurance: usually pay out of pocket by card first (registration + consult + X-ray ≈ €100–300). Be sure to ask for the Feuille de Soins (care sheet) and Facture (invoice) — with these your travel insurer can reimburse you fully back home.

2. Show this to reception/triage:

"Je suis tombé de vélo cet après-midi. Mon genou saigne et mon gros orteil gauche ainsi que ma plante du pied sont très gonflés. Je ne peux pas poser le pied par terre. J'ai besoin d'une radiographie pour vérifier s'il y a une fracture."
(I fell off my bike this afternoon. My knee is bleeding and my left big toe and sole are very swollen. I can't put weight on my foot. I need an X-ray to check for a fracture.)

💡 Before heading in: wear loose slippers or fully-unlaced sneakers (hard shoes crush a swollen foot); have a companion support you, or borrow an umbrella as a makeshift cane.

💬 Prompt
Can a French public hospital (Hôpital Pasteur) charge my US insurance card? How does the cost / reimbursement work?
✦ Gemini

French public hospitals (like Hôpital Pasteur) usually can't directly bill US commercial insurance (Blue Cross, Aetna, Cigna). As a foreigner it's "pay upfront, reimburse at home." But don't worry:

1. French public ER is cheap — it's price-capped. ER + consult + X-ray is typically €100–300 (~$110–330), possibly less than your US deductible; pay with a chipped US card (Visa/Mastercard).

2. Your US plan likely covers "emergency medical abroad" — most good PPO plans do; a bike fall is a textbook emergency. Pay on site and collect the reimbursement docs.

📝 The "reimbursement trio" to request before leaving (at the Bureau des Entrées / Caisse):

  1. Facture Acquittée — the paid invoice.
  2. Feuille de Soins — the care sheet, with official stamp and doctor's signature.
  3. Compte-rendu médical / Radio — the medical report + X-ray (paper, or a card with a link/password to download the digital image).
💡 The travel credit card you booked the flights with often includes Travel Insurance that can reimburse medical costs — even crutches; or call your standalone travel insurer's 24h line.

🛂 The Trip Home: Cross-3-Country, One Transfer Accessibility (WCHR)

The way home was more complex — three countries' airports (one transfer: NCE → LHR → SEA, changing at London Heathrow). The key was arranging wheelchair assistance for the whole route in advance. Aviation has standard IATA special-assistance codes, so I had Gemini clarify which to request and how to talk to ground staff:

  • WCHR (Wheelchair – Ramp): can manage stairs and walk to the seat, just can't walk long distances; needs a wheelchair to/from the gate/jet bridge.
  • WCHS (Wheelchair – Steps): can't manage stairs, but can walk to the seat in-cabin; needs a wheelchair to the aircraft door.
  • WCHC (Wheelchair – Cabin): needs a wheelchair the whole way, including to the seat and in-cabin.

Since there's only one transfer (at London Heathrow, LHR), the key is to request this assistance at booking/check-in and make sure the LHR connection doesn't break down — with just 1 hour, crossing borders (UK→US) and re-clearing transfer security, that's where it most easily goes wrong.

What Gemini helped with

Explained the WCHR / WCHS / WCHC differences and helped me pick which to request (I ended up on WCHR); generated English/French lines to request assistance at Nice check-in and the LHR transfer; flagged the tight 1-hour connection.

💶 Airport tax refund (Détaxe)

💬 Prompt
What can I get refunded on the way home, where, and how?
✦ Gemini

In France (and the EU), the core rule of the tax refund (Détaxe / Tax Free): only "goods" you buy and take home unused qualify; all "services, dining, medical" never do. Sort your receipts:

  • Refundable: clothes, bags, cosmetics, electronics, sealed souvenirs taken home.
  • Not refundable: restaurants, hotels, car/taxi, train tickets, and all the medical costs (Hôpital Pasteur, pharmacy) and the Donjoy boot. (No worries — the medical and boot go on the travel credit card's insurance back home.)

🛍️ The key prerequisite: a plain receipt (Ticket de caisse) can't be refunded at the airport. 📍 Where (recommend Nice NCE): France uses the PABLO electronic refund system. T1 near Gate 2, T2 near Gate 3 (before check-in/security). Find the orange PABLO kiosk, switch to your language, scan the barcode:

  • Green (✓): customs e-stamp done; if you linked a card at purchase, the money returns in a few weeks.
  • Red (✗): manual review — go to the Douane (customs) desk with passport, receipts, and the unopened goods for a hand stamp.

🚨 Safety: the Riviera's "pickpocket ecosystem"

"Watch out, pickpockets."

— a staff member openly warning the waiting crowd, in the NCE passport-control line right after we landed

The South of France is pickpocket territory, so before the trip I also had Gemini explain "why so many thieves" and "how safe Nice really is."

What Gemini helped with
💬 Prompt
Why does the South of France have so many pickpockets?
✦ Gemini

The Riviera (Nice, Marseille, Cannes) is Europe's pickpocket "hot zone" not because locals turned bad, but because a warped "theft ecosystem" formed. A few blunt reasons:

  1. A paradise of rich, relaxed marks: a top resort where wealthy tourists pile in each summer; vacation minds drop their guard; people carry premium cameras, designer bags, the latest iPhones, even cash — "gold everywhere" to a thief.
  2. Easy geography (perfect getaway): one main coastal rail line (TER) — steal in Cannes, fence in Nice 20 min later; the Italian border (Ventimiglia) is ~40 min by train, so cross-border gangs slip across the same night, beyond French police reach.
  3. Very light penalties: non-violent theft is treated leniently — caught without violence usually means a statement and a few hours' hold; gangs bring in minors (hard to sentence); summer police are stretched, so a "lost wallet / balcony climb" is basically logged for the insurance claim.
  4. Old architecture aids the "balcony crews": ornate façades, columns, walkways, and iron balconies are like climbing holds; summer travelers leave balcony doors ajar for air — a green light.
💡 In a line: theft here is "high reward, low cost and risk." But don't panic — these thieves are opportunists who only pick the easiest targets.
💬 Prompt
Is Nice safe overall? What should I watch for?
✦ Gemini

Nice ranks mid-tier among major European tourist cities — generally safe, but watch specific street crime:

🚨 The main risk is theft (violent crime is rare, but pickpocketing, bag- and phone-snatching are common). High-risk spots:

  • The Promenade des Anglais and the narrow streets of Vieux Nice (old town) — pickpocket central.
  • On the beach: never leave your bag or phone while you swim.
  • Nice-Ville central station and around it: shady at night, frequent theft.
  • Late at night on quiet lanes, watch who's behind you.

💡 Tips: don't flash valuables or count large cash in public; wear your bag in front, not on the chair back; be wary of "ask for directions / sign a petition / help with your bag" scams — decline politely and move on; avoid dark side streets alone late at night, and pair up.

Is balcony break-in theft common at Nice hotels? The front desk won't move my room — what do I do tonight?

Honestly, balcony break-ins targeting tourists aren't rare on the Riviera — the South of France has one of France's highest burglary rates. Thieves favor two targets: low floors or rooms with climbable balconies, and relaxed, off-guard tourists.

Typical MOs:

  • Deep-night entry: 2–5 AM over the balcony, quiet, grabbing cash/watch/wallet/phone left on the table.
  • "Balcony grab" while distracted: when you shower or lie in bed on your phone with the balcony ajar, they reach in and hook valuables.

  1. Never leave the balcony door cracked: lock it fully; run the A/C, or endure the heat — most balcony thefts happen through a cracked door.

  3. Set up a noise barrier: wedge a chair or suitcase hard against the balcony door, with a glass upside-down on top.

  • 冷敷（Ice）：冰袋或毛巾裹冰镇饮料敷脚趾，每次 15–20 分钟，一天几次。前两天千万别热敷，会加重肿胀。
  • 抬高（Elevate）：休息时用枕头把脚垫高于心脏，促进血液回流消肿。
  • 减少负重：尽量少走动，别让大拇指用力。

⚠️ 什么时候去医院：若完全无法下地、脚趾明显变形歪斜、或剧痛难忍，去拍 X 光排除骨折/关节脱位。膝盖伤口先用生理盐水或清水冲净再涂碘伏，这两天洗澡别碰水。
